A Bear Problem

Polar bears are staying longer along Arctic coastlines because climate warming is causing sea ice to melt earlier in the spring and form later in the fall, reducing the amount of time they can hunt seals from the ice, which is their primary feeding platform. When the ice retreats far offshore during summer and early autumn, many bears are forced onto land and must remain there until sea ice returns. This extended stay on the coast has several disadvantages: polar bears generally find much less nutritious food on land than they do from hunting seals, so they often lose body weight and energy reserves while fasting; females may have reduced reproductive success and produce fewer or smaller cubs; young bears can experience lower survival rates; and increased time near human settlements can lead to more human–bear conflicts, creating risks for both bears and people. Over time, longer periods spent onshore can contribute to declining health and population challenges for some polar bear populations.

Domino Effect - Arctic Amplification

The loss of sea ice isn't just a symptom of climate change—it's a powerful catalyst. As the reflective white ice disappears, the dark ocean absorbs more solar radiation, causing the Arctic to heat up nearly four times faster than the rest of the planet.

The Critical Months

The summer months of June, July, and August are critical for polar bear survival because they coincide with the annual sea-ice minimum. As Arctic sea ice retreats during this period, many polar bears are forced ashore and use Wrangel Island as a temporary refuge. Bears of all ages may spend weeks or even months on the island waiting for sea ice to reform and restore access to their primary hunting grounds.

Rising Arctic temperatures are shortening the duration of stable sea ice and delaying its return in autumn. As a result, polar bears must remain on land for longer periods, where food sources are limited and far less nutritious than seals. Extended stays onshore force bears to rely on stored fat reserves, increasing the risk of weight loss, reduced reproductive success, and lower cub survival rates.

Climate projections suggest that under a high-emissions scenario, conditions could worsen dramatically by the late twenty-first century. By around 2080, prolonged ice-free seasons may leave Wrangel Island and other Arctic coastlines unable to support large numbers of polar bears for extended periods, threatening the long-term viability of many polar bear populations across the Arctic.
